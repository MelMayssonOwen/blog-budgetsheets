What Is the Formula for Calculating Savings and Interest?

Published 2026-11-07

TL;DR: The basic savings formula is starting balance + contributions + interest earned - withdrawals = ending balance. In a spreadsheet, you can total deposits with SUM, estimate or record interest separately, and use the ending balance formula to check that your savings line matches your zero-based budget.

The basic savings formula

At the spreadsheet level, savings is usually just money set aside minus money taken back out. Put those together, and the basic idea is: ending savings balance = starting balance + contributions + interest earned - withdrawals.

A calculator or spreadsheet can show total contributions, total interest earned, and the ending balance.

What each part of the formula means

Principal is the money already in the savings account or the starting amount you begin with. In a household spreadsheet, it is the balance you are building on.

Contributions are the new deposits you add. This can be a one-time transfer, a recurring monthly transfer, or both.

Interest rate / APY is the rate used to estimate how much the balance grows. APY means annual percentage yield, which is the yearly rate used in many savings examples and calculator tools.

Time is how long the money stays saved. That might be months or years, depending on the goal.

Ending balance is what you have after adding contributions and interest, and subtracting any withdrawals. For a spreadsheet, this is the number that tells you whether the category is actually doing its job, or just sitting there looking responsible.

Spreadsheet formulas you can use

If you want the simple version in a sheet, these formulas are enough to get started.

Say:

  • B2 = starting balance
  • B3 = total contributions
  • B4 = total withdrawals
  • B5 = total interest earned

Then the ending balance formula is:

=B2+B3-B4+B5

If you track each monthly deposit in a column, you can total contributions with a built-in function like:

=SUM(C2:C13)

If you want interest earned as a simple estimate, one common spreadsheet approach is to multiply the average balance by the annual rate and the time fraction. For example:

=(B2+B3/2)*B6*B7

where:

  • B6 = annual interest rate as a decimal, like 0.04
  • B7 = time in years

That is a simplified math model, not a promise of what a bank will pay. If you are using an actual savings account, the APY and the way interest is applied can change the result.

If your spreadsheet has the built-in FV function, you can also use it to estimate future value from a rate, number of periods, and deposits. The exact setup depends on how your sheet is organized, so the plain arithmetic above is often easier to verify with your eyes instead of trusting the formula goblin.

Hypothetical worked example

Here is a hypothetical example you can copy into your own budget sheet.

Assume:

  • Starting balance: $500
  • Monthly savings added for 6 months: $100 each month
  • Simplified interest estimate: 2% annual rate
  • Time: 6 months, or 0.5 years

Step 1, total contributions:

$100 x 6 = $600

Step 2, estimate interest on the average balance. If we use a simple average of starting balance plus half the contributions:

$500 + ($600 / 2) = $800 average balance

Step 3, estimate annual interest for half a year:

$800 x 0.02 x 0.5 = $8

Step 4, calculate ending balance:

$500 + $600 + $8 = $1,108

So the hypothetical ending balance is $1,108.

If you want this in spreadsheet form, you could set it up like this:

  • B2 = 500
  • B3 = 600
  • B5 = 8
  • B8 = =B2+B3+B5

That gives you a clean check on the math, and it is easy to change later when the budget inevitably gets weird because life happens.

How this fits a zero-based budget spreadsheet

In a zero-based budget, savings should be a category with a job, not just a vague promise. You assign every dollar to a line item, including savings, so there is no random leftover cash wandering around unassigned like it paid rent elsewhere.

A practical setup looks like this:

  • Planned savings amount in the budget section
  • Actual transfer recorded in the transactions section
  • Interest tracked as a separate line if the account earns it
  • Ending balance formula in the savings tab

When those pieces match, every dollar is assigned. No leftovers, no mystery money, no spreadsheet folklore.
