What Is Considered Irregular Income?

Published 2026-11-20

TL;DR: Irregular income is money that changes in amount or timing from month to month, such as overtime, commissions, freelance pay, tips, or seasonal work. In a zero-based budget spreadsheet, list only cash already received, then assign the irregular portion after it arrives so the budget stays grounded in real money.

Irregular income is money that changes in amount, timing, or both from month to month. It often includes overtime, commissions, freelance work, tips, seasonal work, bonuses, and self-employment income. In a household budget spreadsheet, the safest approach is to treat that money separately from steady pay and budget only with cash that has actually arrived.

What counts as irregular income

Irregular income is any income source that does not land in the same amount on the same schedule each month. If you cannot rely on the deposit date or the deposit size, it belongs in this bucket.

Common examples include:

  • Overtime pay that depends on extra hours worked
  • Commission income that rises and falls with sales
  • Freelance or contract payments
  • Tips
  • Seasonal work
  • Bonuses that are not part of a fixed paycheck
  • Self-employment income that arrives unevenly

A simple test helps. If you would not expect the same deposit next month, treat it as irregular income in the spreadsheet.

What does not count as irregular income

Not every deposit that looks unusual belongs in the irregular-income bucket.

Usually not irregular income:

  • A regular salary paid on a different day because of a payroll calendar shift

  • A normal paycheck that arrives early or late but is still the same amount

  • Transfers between your own accounts

  • Moving money from savings into checking

  • One-time windfalls, if you are separating them from ongoing household income for budgeting purposes

The point is to separate household earnings from money that is only moving around or showing up once.

Why this matters in a zero-based budget spreadsheet

That works best when the income figure is real.

If you build the whole budget on future commissions or estimated overtime, a slow month can leave categories short even though the spreadsheet looked balanced. That is why guidance on variable income emphasizes budgeting from cash that has actually arrived, not money you expect later.

For a spreadsheet, that usually means you assign dollars after they land.

Hypothetical example: sorting income for a monthly budget

Here is a hypothetical household example.

A household has these income sources for one month:

  • Salary paycheck: $3,000 on the 1st and $3,000 on the 15th
  • Overtime: $240 this month, but not every month
  • Freelance payment: $600 from a project completed in March
  • Childcare reimbursement from an employer: $150
  • Transfer from savings to checking: $500

Step 1: sort the income.

  • Salary paycheck, regular income
  • Overtime, irregular income
  • Freelance payment, irregular income
  • Childcare reimbursement, not household income, because it is money moving back for a cost already paid
  • Transfer from savings, not income, because it is a move between your own accounts

Step 2: total only the actual income you will budget with.

  • Salary: $3,000 + $3,000 = $6,000
  • Overtime: $240
  • Freelance: $600

Budgetable income for the month is the salary plus the irregular income

Step 3: decide how to use the irregular pieces.

If the salary is the stable base, you can place the overtime and freelance money into a holding category, then assign it only after it arrives. That keeps the budget honest when the next month brings less extra pay.

How to record irregular income in the budget

In the income area of the spreadsheet, list only money already received. Do not count a hoped-for commission, a not-yet-paid invoice, or next week’s tips as if they were already in the bank.

A practical setup looks like this:

  • One income line for each source
  • Separate lines or tags for regular and irregular income
  • A month-to-date total based only on actual deposits
  • A holding category for irregular income if you want to park it before assigning it to spending categories

If you track irregular income by source, you can also spot patterns. Maybe freelance income is steady enough to feel predictable. Maybe overtime is all over the place. That distinction matters when you build next month’s plan.

Helpful next step for the budget spreadsheet

Once you identify irregular income, match it with variable expenses, sinking funds, or a holding category so the budget stays balanced when pay changes.

FAQ

Should irregular income be averaged?

You can average it for planning, but the budget should still be based on cash that has actually arrived. Averaging can help you estimate, but it should not replace the real deposit amount in the spreadsheet.

Does a side job count as irregular income?

Usually yes, if the pay varies in amount or timing. If it lands the same amount on the same schedule, it is more like regular income.
