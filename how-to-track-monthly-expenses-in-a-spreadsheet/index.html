How to Track Monthly Expenses in a Spreadsheet

Published 2026-11-01

TL;DR: Track monthly expenses in a spreadsheet by listing your household categories, setting up columns for planned and actual amounts, using formulas to total spending and variance, and reviewing the sheet throughout the month so your budget stays aligned with real costs.

Start with the monthly expense categories

Give every dollar a job if you want the spreadsheet to behave. Begin by listing the household expenses you actually expect each month: housing, utilities, groceries, transportation, debt payments, and those irregular but predictable ones like annual fees or seasonal bills. This gives every transaction a place instead of letting the sheet turn into a tiny financial crime scene.

It is the same core idea behind monthly expense tracking tools and budget templates. Those exist to help you monitor spending and plan monthly costs, not just to admire columns and formulas. A monthly expense tracker might be a spreadsheet, a printable sheet, or something else, but the goal stays the same: track what leaves the account so the budget stops guessing (NoMoreDebts monthly expense tracker).

Set up the spreadsheet structure

Create a simple table with these columns:

  • Date
  • Description
  • Category
  • Planned amount
  • Actual amount
  • Difference

If you are using a template, the official Excel guidance says you can open a template, replace the cell values and naming with your own information, and save or share the finished file.

Use spreadsheet formulas to summarize spending

With the rows ready, add formulas that total each category and compare the plan against reality. Exact function names and syntax vary by tool, so check the official help docs for whatever you are using. For Excel, the formula help for common functions is documented by Microsoft, and the budget template guidance shows how to adapt the sheets to your own spending plan (Excel budget templates).

Typical summary formulas include:

  • Category total, to add all actual amounts in one category
  • Monthly total, to add all actual spending for the month
  • Variance, to compare planned amount and actual amount

A common setup is:

  • Category total = sum of all actual amounts in that category
  • Monthly total = sum of all category totals or all actual amounts in the log
  • Difference = planned amount minus actual amount

That difference column is the one that tells you whether you stayed on plan or wandered off into the land of “I swear this was necessary.”

Work through a hypothetical example

Here is a hypothetical example with one category line, shown step by step.

Say you create a category called Groceries.

  • Planned amount for Groceries: $400
  • Actual spending recorded during the month: $120, then $95, then $110

First, add the actual spending:

  • $120 + $95 = $215
  • $215 + $110 = $325

So the category total is $325.

Now compare that with the planned amount:

  • $400 planned
  • $325 actual
  • Difference = $400 - $325 = $75

That means $75 remains unspent in that category for the month. If you are using a zero-based budget, you would then decide what to do with that $75 instead of letting it sit there looking mysterious and smug.

You can do the same thing for any category, then roll those totals up into the monthly total for the whole household budget.

Review and update the tracker during the month

Enter expenses regularly, not just at the end of the month when you are trying to reconstruct your life from card receipts and memory fragments. The closer you log a transaction to the actual purchase, the easier it is to see whether the plan still matches reality.

As you go, compare the actual amount with the planned amount for each category. If a household cost changes, update the category instead of pretending the spreadsheet will fix it by staring harder. That might mean moving money between categories, revising the planned amount, or adding a new expected expense line if the cost is real and recurring.

This is where the tracker earns its keep. It shows which categories are on track, which ones are running hot, and which ones need a midmonth adjustment before the budget turns into a shrug.

Connect the tracker to the zero-based budget process

A monthly expense tracker can help you track your monthly expenses and compare planned versus actual spending.

That connection matters because the tracker is not just a list of purchases. It is the feedback loop. You plan the money, record the spending, compare planned versus actual, and then use that information to make the next month cleaner and less chaotic.

A good monthly expense tracker does three boring but important things: it shows where money was planned, where it actually went, and what still needs adjusting. That is about as glamorous as budgeting gets, which is honestly fine. Glamour has never paid a utility bill.
