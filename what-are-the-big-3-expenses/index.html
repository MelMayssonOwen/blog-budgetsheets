What Are the Big 3 Expenses?

Published 2026-11-15

TL;DR: The “big 3” expenses usually mean housing, transportation, and food. In a zero-based budget, they are the first categories to plan, track, and compare against actual spending, although some households will swap in other large costs like child care or medical bills.

Housing, transportation, and food

That variation matters. A long commute can make transportation larger than housing. Child care, debt payments, or medical costs can outrank one of the usual three. Consumer-finance guidance also says to account for essentials first, then use the rest of the plan for everything else. The big 3 are a useful shorthand, not a rule. For spreadsheet budgeting, how to track monthly expenses in a spreadsheet and how to start a zero-based budget fit this topic well because they show how to compare planned amounts with actual spending.

Why these three categories matter in a zero-based budget

In a zero-based budget, these are usually the first dollars you assign in the spreadsheet. They recur every month, and they are harder to trim on impulse than a one-off purchase.

Housing, transportation, and food also anchor the rest of the budget. Once they are planned, the remaining categories have a clearer role. A spreadsheet helps because you can compare planned amounts with actual spending instead of guessing where the money went.

What counts in each category

Housing usually means rent or mortgage, plus the costs tied to keeping the home running. That can include utilities and other recurring home costs if you track them there.

Transportation usually includes the car payment, fuel, insurance, transit, parking, and maintenance. If you use more than one form of transportation, the category can get crowded, but the spreadsheet will still total it cleanly.

Food includes groceries and meals out. If you want better control, split them into separate lines. Then you can see whether grocery spending is drifting, dining out is running high, or both are pushing the total up.

Hypothetical worked example: sorting the big 3 in a budget spreadsheet

Here is a hypothetical monthly example using planned, actual, and difference columns.

Example setup

Category Planned Actual Difference
Housing 1,200 1,200 0
Transportation 350 390 -40
Food 500 470 30
Total 2,050 2,060 -10

Step by step arithmetic

  1. Housing planned: 1,200
  2. Transportation planned: 350
  3. Food planned: 500
  4. Total planned for the big 3: 1,200 + 350 + 500 = 2,050

Now the actuals.

  1. Housing actual: 1,200
  2. Transportation actual: 390
  3. Food actual: 470
  4. Total actual for the big 3: 1,200 + 390 + 470 = 2,060

Difference is planned minus actual.

  1. Housing difference: 1,200 - 1,200 = 0
  2. Transportation difference: 350 - 390 = -40
  3. Food difference: 500 - 470 = 30
  4. Total difference: 2,050 - 2,060 = -10

So in this example, transportation ran over by 40, food came in 30 under, and the big 3 ended 10 over plan overall. That is the kind of variance a spreadsheet should catch before the month gets away from you.

How to use the answer in your spreadsheet

A formula-based approach is straightforward: total the three categories, compare that total to take-home pay, and assign every remaining dollar a job so the budget lands at zero. The exact formulas depend on your layout, but the logic stays the same. Sum the category lines, check the balance line, and make sure the remaining amount is fully assigned instead of sitting unplanned.

If your sheet has category rows and a total row, a standard sum formula can add the big 3. If you also track take-home pay, you can subtract total spending from income to confirm the balance is zero after every dollar is assigned.

When the big 3 are not the biggest three

Some households will find child care, debt payments, or medical costs outrank one of the classic big 3. That is normal. Your spreadsheet should reflect real spending, not a generic budget slogan.

If another cost is larger in your household, give it the attention it deserves and customize the category list. The point is to track what is actually happening, not what a neat rule says should happen.

FAQ

Do the “big 3” have an official definition, or are they just a budgeting shorthand?

They are budgeting shorthand. Begin by listing the household expenses you actually expect each month: housing, utilities, groceries, transportation, debt payments, and those irregular but predictable ones like annual fees or seasonal bills.

Should food be split into groceries and dining out in a zero-based budget?

Yes, if that gives you better control. Splitting them can make it easier to see whether grocery spending, eating out, or both are driving the total.
