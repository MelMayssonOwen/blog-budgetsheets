How Much Should I Save Each Month?

Published 2026-11-05

TL;DR: Save the amount your zero-based spreadsheet can assign after essentials, irregular expenses, and goals are funded. In practice, savings is a budget line, not leftover money, and the right monthly amount depends on your income, priorities, and whether the goal is an emergency fund, a sinking fund, or another target.

Intro

The short answer is, save the amount that is still available after you assign essential bills, irregular expenses, and planned goals in a zero-based spreadsheet. In that setup, savings is not whatever is left over after you spend everything else, it is a line item you assign on purpose. The right monthly amount depends on your household’s cash flow and priorities, because your budget has to fit real life, not the fantasy version where the dog never needs a vet visit and the water heater stays calm forever.

What “save each month” means in a zero-based budget

In a zero-based budget, savings is just another category. The math is simple, even if the feelings are not. Every dollar of take-home income gets assigned to something, so savings should not sit around as vague leftover money waiting to be accidentally spent on snacks, sales, or “just this one thing.”

Where that savings line belongs depends on what you are saving for. If the money is meant for a known expense that happens later, like insurance, holiday spending, or a car repair fund, it usually fits in a sinking fund. If the money is there to handle surprise costs, it belongs on an emergency-fund line. If the goal is broader, like building a buffer or funding a future purchase, it can still be a savings line in the same month, as long as it is assigned before the month starts.

How to calculate a monthly savings amount from your spreadsheet

Start with take-home income for the month. Then list fixed costs, variable costs, debt payments, and known irregular expenses. Fixed costs are the steady bills. Variable costs move around a bit. Debt payments are their own obligation. Irregular expenses are the ones that behave like they have a schedule, but only when you are not looking.

Once those are entered, subtract them from monthly income. The remainder is the amount you can assign to savings without breaking the zero-based plan. If the remainder is zero, savings still can be included, but only if another category is reduced. That is the whole trick. The spreadsheet does not care about your vibes, only the totals.

A simple structure is:

  • Take-home income
  • Fixed costs
  • Variable costs
  • Debt payments
  • Known irregular expenses
  • Planned savings assignments

Formula wise, that means:

Savings amount = Income - Fixed costs - Variable costs - Debt payments - Irregular expenses

Hypothetical worked example

Here is a hypothetical example for one household.

  • Take-home income: $4,200
  • Rent: $1,500
  • Utilities: $220
  • Groceries: $600
  • Transportation: $180
  • Debt payment: $300
  • Insurance set-aside: $150
  • Car maintenance sinking fund: $100
  • Holiday sinking fund: $75
  • Emergency savings: $400
  • Other goal savings: $200

Step 1, add the bills and planned set-asides:

$1,500 + $220 + $600 + $180 + $300 + $150 + $100 + $75 + $400 + $200 = $3,725

Step 2, subtract that total from income:

$4,200 - $3,725 = $475

Step 3, assign the remaining $475 to other categories if needed, or increase savings goals. For example, the household could add $275 to an emergency fund and $200 to a home repair sinking fund.

The budget still has to balance to zero, so the final formula is:

Income - all assigned categories = 0

That is the entire point of the zero-based method. Money gets a job before the month starts, and the spreadsheet is the manager nobody asked for but everybody needs.

How to adjust the amount when money is tight or irregular

If income changes from month to month, savings does not have to disappear. It can be lowered, paused, or redirected in the spreadsheet so essentials stay covered first. If the month is tight, reduce the savings line rather than letting the budget go negative and pretending it is fine because future-you will somehow absorb the hit.

When income is irregular, it can help to keep a small, consistent savings line if that is the only workable approach. The amount can be modest as long as it is realistic and repeatable. A tiny line that actually gets funded is better than a heroic target that gets ignored by week two.

The main rule is to revisit the plan each month or each pay period, then move dollars where they are needed most. That keeps savings flexible without turning the spreadsheet into a wish list.

What to put in the spreadsheet so the number stays realistic

To keep the savings amount grounded in reality, add columns for planned, actual, and difference amounts. That way you can see whether the savings line matched the month you expected, or whether life wandered in and kicked over the furniture.

A simple layout might include:

  • Category
  • Planned amount
  • Actual amount
  • Difference

Then add a savings target row, or separate rows for emergency savings and sinking funds if you want to keep those goals distinct. Revisit the planned amount each month based on what actually happened, not what you hoped would happen after three consecutive perfect Mondays.

If you are building out the actual-versus-planned setup, How to Track Monthly Expenses in a Spreadsheet covers the columns and formulas that make those comparisons easier. If you want to keep emergency savings separate from other goals, How Do You Add an Emergency Fund to Your Budget Spreadsheet? explains how to give that line its own place in the budget.

The practical answer, then, is this: save whatever your zero-based spreadsheet can support after essentials, irregulars, and priorities are assigned, then adjust it when your cash flow changes.
