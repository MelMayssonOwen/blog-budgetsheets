Can You Use <> in SUMIFS?

Published 2026-11-10

TL;DR: Yes, you can use `<>` in SUMIFS, but it means “not equal to,” not “less than or greater than.” Use it to exclude a specific label like Transfer or Paid, and use separate criteria such as `">=x"` and `"<x"` when you need a range.

Short answer and why <> is the wrong operator

Yes, you can use <> in SUMIFS, but it does not mean “less than or greater than.” It means not equal to. So <> excludes a specific value rather than testing for two directions at once. Microsoft’s SUMIFS help page shows criteria as text strings in formula arguments: Microsoft Support, SUMIFS function.

How SUMIFS handles comparison criteria

SUMIFS accepts criteria like ">=0", "<100", and "<>Paid" when they are written as text in the criteria argument. The comparison operator sits inside quotes, and the formula evaluates rows that match the condition.

In other words, SUMIFS can filter by:

  • numeric thresholds, like ">=0"
  • upper limits, like "<100"
  • exclusion rules, like "<>Paid"

That matches the official syntax style described in spreadsheet documentation for SUMIFS.

When <> works in a household budget spreadsheet

In a household budget, <> is useful when you want to exclude a label from the total.

For example, you might sum expenses that are not:

  • Transfer
  • Reimbursed
  • Paid

That helps when you are totaling monthly spending and do not want internal transfers or already settled items to affect the result.

What to use instead if you mean a range

If you want “less than or greater than” logic, <> is not the right tool. It is not a numeric range operator. It only means “not equal to.”

For range logic, use separate criteria. For example, if you want to sum only values between two limits, pair criteria like ">=0" and "<100" in the same SUMIFS formula.

That matters in zero-based budgeting, where you may want to sum only the lines that fit a plan. You can set one criterion for the lower bound and another for the upper bound, then let the formula do the filtering.

Hypothetical worked example for a zero-based budget

Here is a hypothetical example.

Suppose a household spreadsheet has these transactions:

Date Category Amount
1/3 Groceries 82
1/5 Utilities 140
1/7 Transfer 200
1/9 Groceries 46
1/12 Dining Out 38
1/15 Transfer 125

You want total spending for the month, but you want to exclude Transfer entries.

A SUMIFS formula could look like this:

=SUMIFS(C2:C7, B2:B7, "<>Transfer")

Step by step:

  • C2:C7 is the amount range.
  • B2:B7 is the category range.
  • "<>Transfer" means include rows where the category is not Transfer.

Now the arithmetic:

  • Groceries: 82
  • Utilities: 140
  • Groceries: 46
  • Dining Out: 38
  • Transfers excluded: 200 + 125

Total included amount:

82 + 140 + 46 + 38 = 306

So the formula returns 306.

That is exactly how <> behaves. It filters out one label. It does not mean “anything except this, but also somehow a range.”

Quick formula patterns to copy

Criteria pattern Meaning Useful when
"<>x" Not equal to x Excluding a label like Transfer or Paid
">=x" At least x Keeping values at or above a floor
"<x" Less than x Keeping values below a limit

In a budget worksheet, these are handy for clean category totals, limit checks, and excluding noise from the totals.
