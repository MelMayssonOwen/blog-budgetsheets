Budgeting glossary: spreadsheet and budgeting terms explained

Updated August 2026

Plain-English definitions of the budgeting and spreadsheet terms used across this site. Where a term has a named source (like the 50/30/20 split or a vendor’s pricing), it’s linked.

50/30/20 rule
A budgeting guideline that splits after-tax income into 50% needs, 30% wants, and 20% savings and debt repayment, as defined by NerdWallet. Popularized by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi (2005).
Zero-based budgeting
A method where every dollar of income is assigned a job until income minus allocations equals zero. The method YNAB is built on (“give every dollar a job”).
Plan vs actual
A format that compares a planned target per category against actual spending, with a variance column (planned minus actual). The default shape of most spreadsheet templates.
Variance
Planned minus actual for a category. Positive means you underspent; negative means you went over.
Bank sync (aggregation)
Automatic import of transactions from your bank and card accounts into a tool. Among spreadsheet options, only Tiller ($99/yr) offers it; free templates require manual entry.
AutoCat
Tiller’s rule-based feature that automatically assigns categories to imported transactions.
Sinking fund
Money set aside gradually for a known future expense (car repair, holidays), so the cost doesn’t hit one month’s budget all at once.
Needs vs wants
The distinction at the heart of 50/30/20: needs are essentials (housing, utilities, groceries); wants are discretionary (dining out, entertainment).
After-tax income
Your take-home pay after taxes and deductions, the figure the 50/30/20 rule and most spreadsheets budget from.
One-time-purchase template
A spreadsheet template you buy once and own, versus a subscription. Typically $9, $29; still manual entry.
Personal-use license
The license on free templates like Vertex42’s: you may use the template but not resell or redistribute it.
SUMIF
A spreadsheet formula that totals values matching a condition, e.g. all “groceries” rows, the workhorse of category totals in a budget sheet.
